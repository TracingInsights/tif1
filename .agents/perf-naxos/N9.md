# N9: cold eager load and `fetch_all`: is it really bandwidth-bound?

## 1. Hypothesis

A cold race `fetch_all_laps_telemetry()` (1128 lap files, 69.2 MB of gzipped
JSON) takes 9.3-10.4 s and an eager `load()` notebook 10-12 s. Earlier
rounds called it bandwidth-bound at "5-8 MB/s per connection with 22
connections", but 22 such connections would move 69 MB in under 2 s. So
either the link (or jsDelivr) caps the aggregate, or tif1's pipeline does.
The task: measure the link outside tif1, instrument tif1's wave and what
follows it, explain the 45.8 s priming run, and fix only what the evidence
shows.

**Answer.** The wave is link-bound: the box's link caps at ~10 MB/s
aggregate (one stream reaches it alone), and tif1's wave runs at that rate.
The "5-8 MB/s per connection x 22" premise was wrong: the 22 requests are
HTTP/2 streams on one connection sharing ~10 MB/s. What is *not*
bandwidth-bound is the 2.5-4 s after the last byte: the payload-tier flush
(~1.35 s of CPU) started only after the gather, fought the GIL-bound frame
build (0.75 s alone, 1.7-2.1 s with the flush beside it, 3-6 s under box
load) and was joined again at exit. N9 moves that flush into the wave, where
the GIL is idle.

## 2. What changed

`src/tif1/async_fetch.py`: `fetch_multiple_async(defer_telemetry_writes=True)`
(the bulk path of `fetch_all_laps_telemetry()`, which an eager `load()` also
takes, and the telemetry batch prefetch) no longer collects the payload-tier
rows into a list that is flushed after the gather. It hands them to a new
`_StreamedTelemetryWrites` sink. The first row starts a writer thread,
registered through `cache.register_frame_writer` like the existing writers
(joined by `Cache.close()`/`invalidate()` and at exit). That thread calls the
same `cache.set_telemetry` per row while the wave is still fetching.

- The writer exits once `close()` was called (in the batch's `finally`) and
  the queue is empty, or after 0.5 s without a row. A later row starts a new
  writer under the sink's lock, so an unclosed sink can never hold the exit
  join.
- Without threads (Pyodide: `register_frame_writer` runs inline) the old list
  plus post-gather flush is kept.
- The lazy stream's caller-owned list (`telemetry_writes=`) is unchanged; it
  already flushes on idle.
- What is written is unchanged: the same `set_telemetry` call per row. A
  cold-primed control cache and candidate cache hold 1128/1128 identical
  payload rows.

Tests: `tests/unit/test_naxos_n9_streamed_writes.py` (8 tests):

- rows land before `close()`;
- `close()` ends the writer at once;
- an unclosed sink does not hold `_join_frame_writers` and restarts on a
  later row;
- write errors are skipped;
- a batch writes its rows during the gather and schedules no post-gather
  flush;
- without threads the list plus post-gather flush remains;
- a failing gather closes the sink.

Probes (in `tools/perf/`, run from the repo root):

- `naxos_n9_link.py`: curl link test.
- `naxos_n9_trace.py`: a cold run with patched-in instrumentation (every
  response body, the batches, the writer threads, per-thread CPU,
  `N9_STACKS=1` stack samples). `--control-src` interleaves the arms and
  reports the network-independent "after last body" metrics.
- `naxos_n9_analyze.py`: prints a trace's timeline.
- `naxos_n9_micro.py`: the post-wave pieces timed serially on warm-cache
  payloads.
- `naxos_n9_cold_bench.py`: a copy of `delphi_cold_bench.py` plus p90, the
  exit tail and raw runs.
- `naxos_n9_cache_diff.py`: a row-level diff of two cold-primed caches.

| four 25 MB streams, speed.cloudflare.com | 100 MB | 10.47 s | 9.6 MB/s aggregate |

| StaticDelivr, 300 files, 22 streams | 18.6 MB | 12.1 s | 1.5 MB/s |

The box's link caps at ~10-11 MB/s whatever the number of connections, so
69 MB cannot arrive in less than ~6.5-7 s. jsDelivr is the right primary;
the fallbacks are 3-7x slower. More connections do not help (64 is slower,
in line with DO_NOT_RETRY).

link rate). The wave itself keeps ~1.0-1.3 cores busy (native decode

The 1.7-2.1 s post-wave phase is the 0.75 s frame build slowed down by the

### (3) The 45.8 s priming run

Re-run as the `prime` workload of `naxos_n9_trace.py` (cold race `load()`,
`fetch_all`, four drivers' telemetry, pandas, at load average ~20-46): 23.6 s
here. Nothing is fetched or built twice: `fetch_all` after the eager load
takes 17 ms (all frames memoized) and the four drivers 0.8 s.

Where the time went:

- the wave: 1.9-15.9 s (14 s at ~5 MB/s: the network was shared with the
  other agents' cold runs);
- after the wave: 5.4 s to `load()`'s return;
- the flush thread: 5.7 s (serial cost 1.35 s; GIL convoy on a loaded box);
- the frame-tier write: 1.9 s;
- the exit join: 1.0 s.

A cold pandas notebook in the same window took 21.2 s, with its flush thread
at 9.5 s. So the 45.8 s is the link (shared with nine other agents priming
or benchmarking) plus the post-wave GIL convoy, which grows with box load.
It is not a second pass.

## 4. Verdict

**SHIP.** The link is the cap for the wave itself: ~10 MB/s whatever the
connection count, measured outside tif1. That part is a valid "stop here"
result; nothing in tif1's wave is slower than curl. The CPU tail after the
wave was not link-bound. N9 removes ~1.0-2.2 s of it on both backends
(network-independent metric: 8/8, 5/8, 7/8, 8/8 pairs, medians -24% to -50%)
without touching the wave (last-body arrival unchanged) or the stored bytes.

On whole-process wall the win shows where the network was steady: polars
notebook -8.8%, 8/8; pandas all_tel -10%, 7/8 in the trace A/B; r1 -7% to
-27%. Round 2's 10-20 s waves drown it, but its p90 improved in every row.
The change is small, behaviour-preserving, and cannot hold the exit (the
writer exits when idle).

## 5. CHANGELOG paragraph

- A cold `fetch_all_laps_telemetry()` (and an eager `load()`) now writes the
  telemetry payloads to the SQLite cache while the download is still
  running, instead of in one flush after the last file arrived. On this
  link (~10 MB/s) the 1128-file wave of a race leaves the CPU mostly idle,
  while the flush (orjson, zstd and SQLite, ~1.35 s of CPU for a race) used
  to fight the frame build for the GIL after the wave and was joined again
  at exit. The work after the last byte drops from 3.9 to 2.5 s (pandas) and
  from 4.3 to 2.2 s (polars), median of 8 interleaved cold runs; a cold
  polars notebook goes from 9.4 to 8.6 s. The cached rows are unchanged.
  Without threads (Pyodide) the flush still runs after the download.

## 6. Notes for the integrator

- **The link, for the record.** Use ~10 MB/s for this box in future rounds,
  not "5-8 MB/s per connection x 22". The 22 requests are HTTP/2 streams on
  one connection, and 22 HTTP/1.1 connections reach the same ~10 MB/s. A
  race's 69 MB of telemetry cannot arrive in less than ~6.5-7 s here.
  Everything left to win on a cold `fetch_all` is the CPU tail after the
  wave (now ~2.2-2.5 s to exit).
- **What remains after the wave** (candidate):
  - the frame build, 0.75 s serial on pandas, 0.82 s on polars;
  - the frame-tier write joined at exit, 0.4-0.6 s;
  - the other atexit hooks plus interpreter finalization, ~0.3 s (N5's
    territory).

  Building the frames during the wave ("streamed/overlapped frame assembly")
  is on DO_NOT_RETRY, from rounds measured on 1-core vantages before the
  GIL-free native decoder. This round's evidence is a wave at ~1 core of 32
  for 7-10 s, with N9 showing a writer thread inside it costs the wave
  nothing. That makes it worth a fresh, gated look (only with ≥ 2 CPUs) in a
  later round. Not attempted here.
- **Further CPU cut, not done.** The served file is exactly
  `{"tel":<payload>}`, and its slice `[7:-1]` is byte-identical to
  `json_dumps_bytes()` of the natively decoded payload (checked on one
  file). Storing that slice would skip the 0.45 s of orjson dumps per race.
  On this box the dumps are already hidden in the wave, so no wall win here.
  It would matter on 1-2 core machines. It needs the raw bytes plumbed to
  `_persist_telemetry_payload` and a verification story for files that are
  not in that exact shape.
- **Behaviour change.** `Cache.invalidate()`/`close()` called from another
  thread during a live bulk wave now waits for that wave's writer, which
  keeps running while rows keep arriving. Before, the post-gather flush
  could re-insert rows after an invalidate. Single-threaded use is
  unaffected.
- **GC.** The streamed writer does not suspend GC (the old flush did, around
  its own loop). `gc.disable()` is process-wide, and "gc.disable during the
  cold fetch" is on DO_NOT_RETRY. The rows are bytes, which GC does not
  track.
- **Measurement conditions.** Every A/B ran at load average 20-46 with
  other agents' cold runs on the same link. The GIL convoy the change
  removes grows with box load, so r1 (-7% to -27%) probably overstates the
  win on a quiet machine. The trace A/B metric is the robust one.
- **Scratch to delete.** `/tmp/naxos/home-N9/cache_ctl` and
  `/tmp/naxos/home-N9/cache_cand` (~140 MB each, the cold-primed caches of
  the write-parity check; my sandbox could not delete outside the tree).
  Also `/tmp/naxos/home-N9/trace_*.json` (traces) and `paths.txt`/`ver10.json`.
- `git commit` was denied in the sandbox: everything is staged, and the
  intended commit messages are in `N9_COMMITS.md`.
